New York

Manhattan, NY

From the cast-iron blocks of SoHo to the brownstones of Harlem — eight neighborhoods, one very dense island, and more history per square foot than almost anywhere in the country. Settled by the Dutch as New Amsterdam in 1624, taken by the English in 1664, and gridded by the Commissioners' Plan of 1811 — today it's the financial, cultural, and media capital most people picture when they say "New York."

Tribeca

Lower Manhattan

The name is short for "Triangle Below Canal Street" — a former warehouse and manufacturing district that artists began converting into loft housing in the 1970s and '80s, the same way SoHo did just to the north. It's quieter and pricier than it looks on a map, all cobblestone and cast-iron facades, and it's had an outsized cultural footprint since Robert De Niro, Jane Rosenthal, and Craig Hatkoff founded the Tribeca Festival in 2001 to help pull Lower Manhattan back on its feet after 9/11 — it's now grown to roughly 150,000 attendees a year.

Brasserie · Since 1980
The Odeon
📍 145 West Broadway

Keith McNally's French-American brasserie put Tribeca on the map for New Yorkers in the first place — the crowd from Saturday Night Live followed it downtown in the '80s, and it's still going strong more than four decades later.

American · Since 1990
Bubby's
📍 120 Hudson St

Started as a pie company out of a tiny kitchen on Hudson Street; now a neighborhood breakfast-and-comfort-food institution that's outlasted most of the restaurants that opened around it.

Annual Festival · June
Tribeca Festival
📍 Venues across Lower Manhattan

More than 600 screenings and roughly 150,000 attendees each June — founded in 2001 as a one-time event to bring people back downtown, it never stopped.

SoHo

South of Houston

SoHo — "South of Houston Street" — holds the largest collection of cast-iron architecture anywhere in the world: roughly 250 surviving cast-iron facades across about 26 blocks, designated the SoHo-Cast Iron Historic District in 1973. Cast iron was cheap to cast into ornate, Corinthian-columned facades that mimicked carved stone, and it let 19th-century manufacturers build enormous window openings for showrooms below and light-filled loft floors above. Artists moved into those lofts, illegally at first, in the 1960s and '70s; the galleries eventually gave way to the flagship stores and boutiques that fill the district now.

French Brasserie · Since 1997
Balthazar
📍 80 Spring St

Keith McNally's second SoHo landmark (after The Odeon) — a big, brass-and-mirror Parisian brasserie that's stayed a scene since it opened, with an adjoining bakery out front.

Bakery · Since 2011
Dominique Ansel Bakery
📍 189 Spring St

Where pastry chef Dominique Ansel invented the Cronut in May 2013 — a croissant-doughnut hybrid that sold out in minutes and got copied worldwide within weeks.

Architecture
SoHo-Cast Iron Historic District
📍 Roughly Canal to Houston, Crosby to West Broadway

Walk Greene Street or Broadway for the concentration of 1840s–1890s cast-iron facades that gives the neighborhood its name and its National Register listing.

Greenwich Village

The Village

New York's bohemian capital for over a century — the birthplace of American folk and beat culture, and of the modern LGBTQ+ rights movement. The Stonewall Inn at 51–53 Christopher Street was the site of the June 1969 uprising that followed a police raid; in 2016 it became the country's first national monument dedicated to LGBTQ+ history. Washington Square Park, ringed by New York University, has been the neighborhood's gathering point since the 19th century, and the crooked, tree-lined streets around it still don't follow the numbered grid that took over the rest of the island in 1811.

Jazz Club · Since 1935
Village Vanguard
📍 178 Seventh Ave South

The oldest operating jazz club in New York City — a basement room that hosted Bill Evans and John Coltrane's most famous engagements. The Vanguard Jazz Orchestra has played every Monday night since 1966.

Jazz Club · Since 1981
Blue Note
📍 131 W 3rd St

An intimate, upscale room that's hosted Dizzy Gillespie, Dave Brubeck, and Wynton Marsalis alongside up-and-coming acts since founder Danny Bensusan opened it in 1981.

Live Music Bar · 40+ Years
The Red Lion
📍 151 Bleecker St

A Bleecker Street rock bar with a band on stage seven nights a week, 7 PM to close — blues, classic rock, soul and funk covers, no cover charge Sunday through Thursday.

University Performing Arts Center
NYU Skirball Center
📍 566 LaGuardia Pl

NYU's 850-seat theater near Washington Square Park runs two public seasons a year of music, dance, theater and comedy — open to anyone, not just students.

Annual Parade · Oct 31
Village Halloween Parade
📍 Sixth Ave, from Spring St to 16th St

Started in 1974 by puppeteer Ralph Lee with a small march through the neighborhood — now the world's largest Halloween parade, with tens of thousands of costumed marchers and roughly two million spectators.

East Village

Alphabet City & St. Marks

Once the eastern edge of Greenwich Village, the East Village became its own identity in the 1960s counterculture years. A century earlier it was "Kleindeutschland" — Little Germany — the largest German-speaking neighborhood outside Central Europe, later home to waves of Russian, Ukrainian, and Polish immigrants; St. Marks Place still carries that layered immigrant history in its storefronts. Tompkins Square Park, opened in 1834, has been ground zero for the neighborhood's protests and riots more than once, most famously in 1988.

Ukrainian Diner · Since 1954
Veselka
📍 144 Second Ave

Opened as a candy store by Ukrainian refugees, then grew into a 24-hour diner serving pierogi and borscht to generations of East Villagers — now run by the founders' grandson.

Concert Venue
Webster Hall
📍 125 E 11th St

A four-story, 2,500-capacity venue with a Grand Ballroom and several smaller rooms — one of the city's premier stops for touring acts and club nights alike.

Park · Est. 1834
Tompkins Square Park
📍 Ave A to Ave B, 7th to 10th St

Roughly 450 elm trees planted after an 1878 redesign still shade the park's paths — the neighborhood's front yard since before the Civil War.

Chelsea

Galleries & the High Line

A former industrial waterfront reinvented twice over. Chelsea Market, on Ninth Avenue, occupies the 1890s National Biscuit Company complex where the Oreo was invented; it's now a food hall threaded by the High Line, the elevated freight rail line that closed in 1980, sat derelict for two decades, and reopened in 2009 as an elevated park running from Gansevoort Street up to Hudson Yards at 34th. West Chelsea's old factory blocks, roughly 18th to 28th Street between Tenth and Eleventh Avenues, became the city's dominant art-gallery district starting in the late 1990s and still are.

Elevated Park · Opened 2009
The High Line
📍 Gansevoort St to W 34th St

A mile and a half of gardens, art installations, and river views built on a defunct 1930s freight line — one of the most-copied park ideas in the world since it opened.

Food Hall · Since 1997
Chelsea Market
📍 75 Ninth Ave

Built inside the old Nabisco factory complex — where the Oreo cookie was first produced — the ground-floor concourse is now dozens of vendors under one roof.

Art District
Chelsea Arts District
📍 18th–28th St, 10th–11th Ave

The city's densest concentration of contemporary art galleries, most with free admission and rotating shows most weeks of the year.

Arena
Madison Square Garden
📍 4 Pennsylvania Plaza

The city's marquee arena, home to the Knicks and Rangers and a regular stop for the biggest touring concerts — Gorillaz and Doja Cat are both booked here later this year.

Upper West Side

Lincoln Center & the Museum

A residential neighborhood between the Hudson River and Central Park, built up around two of the city's cultural anchors: Lincoln Center, the performing-arts campus that's home to the Metropolitan Opera and New York Philharmonic, and the American Museum of Natural History, a complex of 21 interconnected buildings holding 45 permanent exhibition halls plus a planetarium and research library. The blocks around Broadway in the 70s and 80s still run on a handful of century-old food institutions rather than turnover.

Appetizing Store · Since 1934
Zabar's
📍 2245 Broadway (at 80th St)

Four generations of the Zabar family running the same smoked-fish-and-coffee institution — as much a neighborhood landmark as anything on a map.

Bakery · Since 1995
Levain Bakery
📍 167 W 74th St

The original location of the six-ounce chocolate-chip-walnut cookie that launched a national chain — still a line-out-the-door basement bakery on a residential block.

Museum
American Museum of Natural History
📍 Central Park West at 79th St

21 interconnected buildings, 45 exhibition halls, and a planetarium — one of the largest natural history museums on Earth.

Theatre · Since 1929
Beacon Theatre
📍 2124 Broadway

A 2,894-seat Beaux-Arts movie palace turned National Historic Landmark — one of the city's best-loved rooms for touring concerts, with shows most weeks of the year.

Harlem

The Capital of Black America

The center of African American culture, politics, and the arts since the Great Migration, and home to the Harlem Renaissance of the 1920s and '30s. The Apollo Theater, opened in 1934, launched Amateur Night the same year — Ella Fitzgerald won it at fifteen — and the tradition continues today. The Studio Museum in Harlem, the leading institution for Black artists in the country, reopened in November 2025 in a new $160-million, seven-floor building on 125th Street designed by Adjaye Associates.

Soul Food · Since 1962
Sylvia's Restaurant
📍 328 Malcolm X Blvd

Founded by Sylvia Woods, "The Queen of Soul Food," in a 35-seat storefront — now a nearly block-long Harlem institution that's fed everyone from neighborhood regulars to visiting presidents.

American
Red Rooster
📍 310 Lenox Ave

Chef Marcus Samuelsson's restaurant, named for a legendary Harlem speakeasy that once hosted Duke Ellington and James Baldwin — comfort food and live music on Lenox Avenue.

Annual Festival · Aug 1–16
Harlem Week
📍 Citywide, centered on Harlem

Started as a single day in 1974 to lift the neighborhood's spirits during a hard decade; the 2026 edition marked its 52nd straight year, still built around "A Great Day in Harlem" and Harlem Day.

Financial District

FiDi & Wall Street

The oldest settled part of Manhattan and, since the 1792 Buttonwood Agreement organized 24 stockbrokers under a tree on Wall Street, the financial capital of the country. Federal Hall, at Wall and Nassau, is where George Washington took the first presidential oath in 1789. A few blocks of cobblestone on Stone Street — the Stone Street Historic District — are what's left of Dutch-era New Amsterdam. The 9/11 Memorial & Museum, at the World Trade Center site, anchors the district's more recent history.

Tavern & Museum · Since 1762
Fraunces Tavern
📍 54 Pearl St

The oldest building in Manhattan — George Washington's watering hole and the site of his farewell address to his officers in 1783. Still a working restaurant, with a museum on the upper floors.

Steakhouse · Dating to 1837
Delmonico's
📍 56 Beaver St

Widely credited as one of America's first fine-dining restaurants. Closed during the pandemic in 2020 and reopened at its historic Beaver Street address in September 2023.

Memorial & Museum
9/11 Memorial & Museum
📍 180 Greenwich St

The outdoor Memorial plaza is free, no reservation required; the underground museum, built around the World Trade Center's original foundations, requires a timed ticket.
