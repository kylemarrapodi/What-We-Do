New York

Manhattan, NY

From the cast-iron blocks of SoHo to the brownstones of Harlem — thirty-two neighborhoods, one very dense island, and more history per square foot than almost anywhere in the country. Settled by the Dutch as New Amsterdam in 1624, taken by the English in 1664, and gridded by the Commissioners' Plan of 1811 — today it's the financial, cultural, and media capital most people picture when they say "New York."

Battery Park City

Planned on the Hudson

A 92-acre neighborhood that didn't exist until New York built it: the land is Hudson River fill, much of it rock and soil excavated for the original World Trade Center starting in the late 1960s, laid out under a 1969 master plan. Today it's a leafy, family-heavy enclave wrapped by a waterfront esplanade and a 36-acre park system run by the Battery Park City Authority, with the Statue of Liberty in view from most of it.

Civic Center

Courts & City Hall

The seat of New York's government since City Hall opened in 1812 — the oldest city hall in the country still in continuous government use — ringed by a century's worth of monumental courthouses around Foley Square. The neighborhood also holds one of the most important archaeological finds of the 20th century: in 1991, construction at 290 Broadway uncovered a colonial-era burial ground where the remains of 419 free and enslaved Africans were exhumed, then reinterred on the site in 2003.

Tribeca

Lower Manhattan

The name is short for "Triangle Below Canal Street" — a former warehouse and manufacturing district that artists began converting into loft housing in the 1970s and '80s, the same way SoHo did just to the north. It's quieter and pricier than it looks on a map, all cobblestone and cast-iron facades, and it's had an outsized cultural footprint since Robert De Niro, Jane Rosenthal, and Craig Hatkoff founded the Tribeca Festival in 2001 to help pull Lower Manhattan back on its feet after 9/11 — it's now grown to roughly 150,000 attendees a year.

SoHo

South of Houston

SoHo — "South of Houston Street" — holds the largest collection of cast-iron architecture anywhere in the world: roughly 250 surviving cast-iron facades across about 26 blocks, designated the SoHo-Cast Iron Historic District in 1973. Cast iron was cheap to cast into ornate, Corinthian-columned facades that mimicked carved stone, and it let 19th-century manufacturers build enormous window openings for showrooms below and light-filled loft floors above. Artists moved into those lofts, illegally at first, in the 1960s and '70s; the galleries eventually gave way to the flagship stores and boutiques that fill the district now.

Chinatown

Mott, Pell & Doyers

One of the oldest Chinese communities in the United States, rooted since the late 1800s in the crooked blocks of Mott, Pell, and Doyers Streets. Columbus Park, the neighborhood's living room, sits on the site of Mulberry Bend — the most notorious corner of the Five Points slum, demolished in 1897 and replaced with a Calvert Vaux-designed park. Nom Wah Tea Parlor, pouring tea on Doyers Street since 1920, is the neighborhood's oldest continuously running restaurant.

Little Italy

Mulberry Street

Once a sprawling Italian immigrant quarter, today's Little Italy is concentrated along Mulberry Street between Canal and Broome — but what it's lost in size it makes up in staying power, with food businesses run by the same families for four and five generations. The Feast of San Gennaro, held every September since 1926, marks its 100th edition September 17–27, 2026.

Nolita

North of Little Italy

The name is 1990s real-estate shorthand for "North of Little Italy," but the neighborhood is much older than the label: it grew up around St. Patrick's Old Cathedral, the seat of New York's Catholic archdiocese from 1815 until the Fifth Avenue cathedral opened in 1879. The old parish blocks of Elizabeth, Mott, and Mulberry are now lined with boutiques and cafés, with a few genuine old-guard institutions holding their corners.

Lower East Side

The Immigrant Gateway

For over a century the Lower East Side was America's front door — waves of German, Eastern European Jewish, Italian, Puerto Rican, and Chinese arrivals packed into its tenement blocks, a history the Tenement Museum now interprets apartment by apartment at 97 Orchard Street. The food institutions those immigrants founded are still the neighborhood's anchors, side by side with the galleries, music venues, and hotels of its newer life.

Greenwich Village

The Village

New York's bohemian capital for over a century — the birthplace of American folk and beat culture, and of the modern LGBTQ+ rights movement. The Stonewall Inn at 51–53 Christopher Street was the site of the June 1969 uprising that followed a police raid; in 2016 it became the country's first national monument dedicated to LGBTQ+ history. Washington Square Park, ringed by New York University, has been the neighborhood's gathering point since the 19th century, and the crooked, tree-lined streets around it still don't follow the numbered grid that took over the rest of the island in 1811.

Meatpacking District

Gansevoort Market

A few cobblestoned blocks around Gansevoort Street that held some 250 slaughterhouses and packing plants at their 1900 peak — the wholesale meat trade hung on here into the 2000s, long enough to overlap with the district's second life of fashion houses, clubs, and galleries. The Gansevoort Market Historic District was designated in 2003, the Whitney Museum moved to the foot of the High Line in 2015, and the old market square is now one of the city's densest concentrations of rooftops and river views.

East Village

Alphabet City & St. Marks

Once the eastern edge of Greenwich Village, the East Village became its own identity in the 1960s counterculture years. A century earlier it was "Kleindeutschland" — Little Germany — the largest German-speaking neighborhood outside Central Europe, later home to waves of Russian, Ukrainian, and Polish immigrants; St. Marks Place still carries that layered immigrant history in its storefronts. Tompkins Square Park, opened in 1834, has been ground zero for the neighborhood's protests and riots more than once, most famously in 1988.

Stuyvesant Town

The 80 Acres

Eighteen thousand apartments in 110 near-identical brick towers, built by Metropolitan Life in 1947 as housing for returning WWII veterans — the definitive American "towers in the park" superblock, running from First Avenue to Avenue C between 14th and 20th Streets. It's private property, but the tree-lined paths are freely walkable and converge on the Oval, the lawn-and-fountain green at the center. Most of the complex's amenities — gyms, lounges, the screening room — are for residents only; what's listed here is what a visitor can actually use.

NoHo

North of Houston

A pocket-sized district of loft blocks around Lafayette Street and Broadway, named — like its neighbor across Houston Street — by real-estate shorthand: "North of Houston." Its showpiece is Colonnade Row, the marble Corinthian colonnade of 1831–33 that survives from the era when Lafayette Place was the city's most fashionable address; a few doors up, the Public Theater packs six stages into one landmark building.

Gramercy Park

The Private Square

The neighborhood radiates from its namesake: two manicured acres that remain famously private — access is by key, reserved for residents of the surrounding buildings, while everyone else admires the park through its iron fence. Around the square stand two great Victorian-era clubhouses, The Players and the National Arts Club, and a block east on 18th Street is the tavern that claims the title of New York's oldest continuously operating bar.

Across the street, Madison Square Park anchors the district: a free public park with a rotating public-art program and the ivy-covered kiosk where Shake Shack began.

Kips Bay

Curry Hill & the East 20s–30s

The name reaches back to Jacobus Kip, whose Dutch-era farmland met the East River at a bay long since filled in. Today it's an unflashy, livable stretch of the East Side — a big multiplex on Second Avenue, the spice-and-curry corridor along Lexington that New Yorkers call Curry Hill, and a landmark 1900s public bathhouse that still holds city swimming pools.

Murray Hill

The Morgan's Neighborhood

Best known as the home of Pierpont Morgan's private library — now the Morgan Library & Museum — the East 30s keep a lower profile than the districts around them. The rewards are tucked away: a Civil War-era stable mews off 36th Street, a Nordic cultural center on Park Avenue, and a Jewish deli that's been slicing pastrami on Third Avenue since 1964.

Chelsea

Galleries & the High Line

A former industrial waterfront reinvented twice over. Chelsea Market, on Ninth Avenue, occupies the 1890s National Biscuit Company complex where the Oreo was invented; it's now a food hall threaded by the High Line, the elevated freight rail line that closed in 1980, sat derelict for two decades, and reopened in 2009 as an elevated park running from Gansevoort Street up to Hudson Yards at 34th. West Chelsea's old factory blocks, roughly 18th to 28th Street between Tenth and Eleventh Avenues, became the city's dominant art-gallery district starting in the late 1990s and still are.

Elevated Park · Opened 2009
The High Line
📍 Gansevoort St to W 34th St

A mile and a half of gardens, art installations, and river views built on a defunct 1930s freight line.

Food Hall · Since 1997

Built inside the old Nabisco factory where the Oreo cookie was first produced — dozens of vendors under one roof.

Art District

The city's densest concentration of contemporary art galleries, most with free admission.

Garment District

Fashion Avenue

The blocks west of Broadway in the 30s once produced most of the clothing made in America, and Seventh Avenue is still co-signed "Fashion Avenue" through the district. The trade survives at a fraction of its old size — showrooms, trimming shops, and fabric houses upstairs — while Macy's has anchored the district's Herald Square corner since 1902. Judith Weller's bronze "Garment Worker," at a sewing machine on Seventh Avenue since 1984, is the neighborhood's memorial to the trade in plain sight.

Department Store · Since 1902

📍 151 W 34th St

The 2.2-million-square-foot Beaux-Arts flagship that was the world's largest store for decades — ride the 1920s Otis wooden escalators, the last of their kind still running.

Hudson Yards

The Far West Side

Manhattan's newest neighborhood, opened in 2019 on a deck built over the working West Side rail yards — the largest private real-estate development in American history. It's a vertical district of supertalls, a seven-story retail-and-dining complex, and a public square that collects most of the area's headline attractions within a couple of blocks of the High Line's northern end.

Hell's Kitchen

Ninth Avenue & Restaurant Row

The tenement district west of Eighth Avenue kept its dockworker grit long after the piers went quiet, and its official alias "Clinton" never really stuck. Today it runs on two engines: the wall-to-wall international restaurants of Ninth Avenue, and the pre-theater strip of West 46th Street between Eighth and Ninth known as Restaurant Row, feeding Broadway audiences a block from the theaters.

Midtown West

Times Square & the Theater District

The blocks between Fifth and Eighth Avenues in the 40s and 50s hold the densest collection of landmarks in the city: Times Square — renamed in 1904 when The New York Times moved in — the Broadway theaters, Rockefeller Center, and 57th Street's concert halls. Roughly 41 Broadway houses operate within a few blocks of Duffy Square, making this the largest commercial theater district in the world.

Midtown East

Grand Central & Fifth Avenue

East of Fifth Avenue, midtown belongs to the railroad: Grand Central Terminal opened in 1913 and the office district called "Terminal City" grew up over its buried tracks, later joined by the Chrysler Building's stainless-steel crown and, in 2020, the supertall One Vanderbilt next door. St. Patrick's Cathedral has faced Rockefeller Center across Fifth Avenue since the center rose in the 1930s — the cathedral itself opened in 1879.

Tudor City

The Cliff-Top Enclave

Fred F. French's 1920s apartment colony on the bluff above East 42nd Street — a dozen Tudor Revival brick towers built as a self-contained "city within a city," reached by staircases from 42nd and crowned by the rooftop TUDOR CITY sign that still glows over the street. The enclave deliberately turned its back on the East River (the shore below held slaughterhouses at the time — the same blocks the United Nations occupies today).

Private Parks · Open to All

📍 Tudor City Pl, between E 41st & E 43rd

Two manicured, volunteer-kept gardens at the heart of the enclave — privately owned but open to the public daily, roughly 7 AM to 10 PM.

The famous photographers' perch: 42nd Street running arrow-straight toward the Chrysler Building — and the prime spot for Manhattanhenge sunsets each May and July.

A 12-story glass atrium holding a subtropical garden of nearly 40 species, plus a free art gallery — open to the public weekdays 11 AM–6 PM, with pre-registration required by 5 PM the day before.

Dry-aged steaks and a dark-wood dining room inside the enclave itself — lunch weekdays, dinner nightly.

Turtle Bay

The UN's Neighborhood

Named for a long-vanished cove on the East River, Turtle Bay traded its slaughterhouses for world diplomacy when the United Nations built its headquarters on the riverfront in 1947–52. Behind the missions and consulates it stays surprisingly residential — Katharine Hepburn lived on East 49th Street for over 60 years, in the brownstone enclave of Turtle Bay Gardens.

Sutton Place

Quiet Money on the River

A few short blocks of riverfront calm between 53rd and 59th Streets east of First Avenue, where the avenue itself changes name to Sutton Place — for Effingham Sutton, the shipping merchant who built brownstones here in 1875. It turned fashionable around 1920, when Anne Morgan and Anne Vanderbilt led society's move into townhouses on the river, and it has stayed one of the city's most discreet addresses ever since — the payoff for visitors is a string of small parks hanging over the East River with the Queensboro Bridge filling the sky.

📍 Sutton Pl at E 57th St

A brick-paved terrace over the FDR with a bronze wild boar (a Florence "Porcellino" cast) and a full-frame view of the Queensboro Bridge — the best of the little parks dotted along the river from 53rd to 57th.

Movie-Poster View

Roosevelt Island

The Island in the River

A two-mile sliver in the East River that has been, in turn, Blackwell family farmland, the city's quarantine-and-asylum island, a 1970s "new town in town," and now home to Cornell Tech's graduate campus. It's the only Manhattan neighborhood you can reach by aerial tramway — and its car-light waterfront promenades face the skyline head-on. (The island's lone hotel, the Graduate, closed in November 2025.)

📍 Boards at Second Ave & E 59th St

Four minutes over the East River beside the Queensboro Bridge — a standard $3 OMNY/MetroCard fare, running 6 AM–2 AM (to 3:30 AM Fri–Sat), every 7–15 minutes.

Lincoln Square

The Performing-Arts Campus

The blocks around Lincoln Center, rebuilt wholesale in the 1950s–60s when Robert Moses' urban renewal cleared the San Juan Hill neighborhood (the setting of "West Side Story") for a marble campus of opera, symphony, ballet, and conservatory. More world-class performance happens per acre here than anywhere else in the country.

Harlem

The Capital of Black America

The center of African American culture, politics, and the arts since the Great Migration, and home to the Harlem Renaissance of the 1920s and '30s. The Apollo Theater, opened in 1934, launched Amateur Night the same year — Ella Fitzgerald won it at fifteen. The historic theater is currently closed for its first major renovation, with programming (and Amateur Night, once it resumes) running at the Apollo Stages at the Victoria, just down 125th Street, until the main stage reopens. The Studio Museum in Harlem, the leading institution for Black artists in the country, reopened in November 2025 in a new $160-million, seven-floor building on 125th Street designed by Adjaye Associates.

Financial District

FiDi & Wall Street

The oldest settled part of Manhattan and, since the 1792 Buttonwood Agreement organized 24 stockbrokers under a tree on Wall Street, the financial capital of the country. Federal Hall, at Wall and Nassau, is where George Washington took the first presidential oath in 1789. A few blocks of cobblestone on Stone Street — the Stone Street Historic District — are what's left of Dutch-era New Amsterdam. The 9/11 Memorial & Museum, at the World Trade Center site, anchors the district's more recent history.
